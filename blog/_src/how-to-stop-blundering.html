You know a knight forks. You know a pinned piece can't move. You still lose a rook to it twice a week. That's not a knowledge problem, it's a checking problem, and checking is a habit you can build.

Why you blunder (it's not that you're bad at chess)

Look at your last ten losses. In most of them you'd have spotted the losing move as a puzzle in two seconds. You didn't see it during the game because you weren't looking: you were busy with your own plan. Blunders come from three places:

  • Tunnel vision. You calculate your attack and forget your opponent also gets a move.
  • Relaxing after a good stretch. Ten safe moves in a row and you stop checking the eleventh.
  • Time and tilt. Fast time controls, low clock, or the game after a bad loss.

The 10-second safety check

Before you play a move — after you've chosen it, before you touch the piece — ask three questions:

  1. What does their last move attack? Every enemy move changes something. Find it.
  2. After my move, what's undefended? Look at every one of your pieces, especially the one that just moved and the ones it was protecting.
  3. What are their checks and captures? Forcing moves first. If one of them wins material, don't play your move.

It feels slow for the first week. Then it becomes automatic, the same way checking mirrors becomes automatic when you learn to drive. Play rapid (10 minutes or longer) while you're building the habit; it's almost impossible to learn in bullet.

Sit on your hands. Old advice, still the best: decide your move, then do the safety check, then play it. Never the other way round.

The five blunders that cost the most rating

1. The hanging piece

A piece that's attacked and not defended. The most common blunder at every level under 1200. Fix: question 2 of the check, every move.

2. The knight fork

Knights attack in an L that's easy to miss, especially against king and queen or two rooks. Fix: whenever an enemy knight is two jumps from your king and queen, look at its squares.

3. The pin you walked into

Moving a piece onto a line with your king or queen behind it. Fix: before putting a piece in front of your king or queen, check for enemy bishops, rooks and queens on that line.

4. The back-rank mate

Your king is tucked behind three unmoved pawns and a rook lands on your back rank. Fix: once the position opens up, give your king an escape square (often h3 or h6).

5. Taking the poisoned pawn

A free pawn that isn't free. Fix: before any capture, ask what the capturing piece leaves undefended and what it walks into.

How to train it out

  • Review every loss for the blunder, not the opening. Name the type (from the five above). After two weeks you'll know your personal top two.
  • Do puzzles on your top two themes. Ten a day, on the theme you keep losing to, beats fifty random ones.
  • Solve from the defender's side. After each puzzle, go back one move and find the move that allowed it. That's the move you need to stop playing.
  • Stop after two losses in a row. Blunder rates jump when you're tilted. More on that in how to stop tilting.

FAQ

Will I ever stop blundering completely?

No. Grandmasters blunder too. The goal is blundering less often than your opponents, which at most levels is enough to gain hundreds of points.

Should I play slower time controls?

While you're building the habit, yes. Rapid gives you time to do the check every move. Once it's automatic it carries over to blitz.

Are puzzles enough?

Puzzles teach you to see tactics when you know one exists. The safety check teaches you to look when you don't. You need both.
